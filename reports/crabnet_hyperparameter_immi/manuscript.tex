\abstract{Benchmarks are crucial for driving progress in scientific
disciplines. To be effective, benchmarks should closely mimic real-world
tasks while being computationally efficient, allowing for accessibility
and repeatability. Developing surrogate models that can be
indistinguishable from the ground-truth observation within the explored
dataset bounds dramatically reduces the computational burden of running
benchmarks without sacrificing quality, but this requires a large amount
of initial data. In the fields of materials science and chemistry,
relevant optimization tasks can be challenging due to their complexity,
which includes hierarchical, noisy, multi-fidelity, multi-objective,
high-dimensional, and non-linearly correlated variables. Additionally,
they may include mixed numerical and categorical variables that are
subject to linear and non-linear constraints. Simulating or
experimentally verifying such tasks can be difficult, which is why
benchmarks are essential. This study aimed to overcome these challenges
by generating 173,219 quasi-random hyperparameter combinations across
23 hyperparameters and using them to train CrabNet on the Matbench
experimental band gap dataset (computational runtime: 387 RTX-2080-Ti
GPU days). The results were stored in a free-tier shared MongoDB Atlas
dataset, creating a regression dataset that maps hyperparameter
combinations to metrics such as MAE, RMSE, computational runtime, and
model size. Heteroskedastic noise was incorporated into the regression
dataset, and bad hyperparameter combinations were excluded; percentile
ranks were computed within each group of identical parameter sets to
capture heteroskedastic noise rather than assuming Gaussian noise. This
approach can be applied to other benchmark datasets, bridging the gap
between optimization benchmarks with low computational overhead and
realistically complex, real-world optimization scenarios.}

\keywords{Bayesian optimization, benchmark dataset, CrabNet,
  high-dimensional optimization, hyperparameter optimization,
  multi-fidelity}

\section{Background \& Summary}\label{sec:background}

Industry-relevant optimization tasks in the physical sciences are often
``hierarchical, noisy, multi-fidelity~\cite{Existing01_Ghoreishi2019,
Existing02_Kandasamy2020_Dragonfly, SabanzaGil2025_MFBO,
Gantzler2023_MFBO_COF}, multi-objective~\cite{Existing03_Hanaoka2022,
Existing04_Hase2018_Chimera, Ament2023_LogEI}, high-dimensional~\cite{Existing05_Baird2022_CrabNet,
Existing06_Eriksson2021_SAASBO, Hvarfner2024_VanillaBO,
Papenmeier2023_Bounce}, and non-linearly correlated while
exhibiting mixed numerical and categorical variables subject to
linear~\cite{Existing07_09_Baird2023_Compactness} and non-linear
constraints.''~\cite{Existing08_Baird2023_HardSphere,
Existing07_09_Baird2023_Compactness, Hickman2025_Anubis} Existing
benchmark datasets and platforms for materials and molecular
optimization~\cite{Existing10_Dunn2020_Matbench,
Existing11_DeBreuck2021_MODNet, Choudhary2024_JARVIS,
Existing12_Wang2022_ActiveLearning, Existing13_Liang2021_BO_Benchmarks,
Existing14_Henderson2021, Existing15_Hase2021_Olympus,
Hickman2025_Atlas}, while very useful, typically are single-objective,
single-fidelity, low-dimensional, and ignore or simplify the influence
of noise. The growing population of self-driving
laboratories~\cite{Tom2024_SDL_Review, Abolhasani2023_SDL} and
attention-based composition models that succeed
CrabNet~\cite{Madani2025_TransformerGraph} therefore have few publicly
available surrogates against which their adaptive-design components
can be exercised under realistic, hierarchical, noisy conditions.

To address this gap, we generated a large hyperparameter response
surface for the original CrabNet composition-only band gap regression
model. A quasi-random Sobol design~\cite{Existing02_Kandasamy2020_Dragonfly}
of 65{,}536 hyperparameter combinations was specified over a
constrained search space spanning 23 numerical and categorical
hyperparameters; each combination was repeated up to five times,
yielding 327{,}680 scheduled training runs. Of these, 173{,}219
completed successfully on RTX-2080-Ti GPUs (387 GPU-days,
$\sim$4{,}614 CUDA-core years), producing 41{,}550 unique parameter
sets with 1--5 successful repeats each. The resulting regression
dataset records four observed outputs --- mean absolute error (MAE),
root-mean-square error (RMSE), GPU runtime, and on-disk model size ---
for every successful run, and is released together with a trained
surrogate so that downstream users can evaluate optimization
strategies without re-running the underlying CrabNet training.

The dataset and surrogate have already been re-used to benchmark
algorithms in the BayBE library~\cite{Fitzner2025_BayBE} and the Ax
platform via an interactive HuggingFace
Space\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/spaces/AccelerationConsortium/crabnet-hyperparameter}}
and in an Acceleration Consortium Kaggle
competition\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/acceleration-consortium/crabnet-optimization-challenge-dataset}},
demonstrating that the dataset described here can serve as a realistic
yet computationally cheap testbed for the broader BO and SDL community.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Hyperparameter search space and sampling design}
\label{sec:methods-search-space}

The 23 hyperparameters span CrabNet's encoder configuration (e.g.,
\texttt{N}, \texttt{d\_model}, \texttt{heads}, \texttt{dim\_feedforward},
\texttt{dropout}), training schedule (\texttt{epochs\_step}, \texttt{lr},
\texttt{weight\_decay}, \texttt{batch\_size}, optimizer betas), data
augmentation / scaling switches (\texttt{emb\_scaler}, \texttt{pos\_scaler},
\texttt{eps}, \texttt{fudge}, \texttt{alpha}), and categorical choices
(\texttt{criterion}, \texttt{elem\_prop}, \texttt{bias},
\texttt{out\_hidden4}). Continuous parameters were given physically
meaningful upper and lower bounds, with mutually-incompatible
configurations excluded a priori so that all sampled combinations are
admissible. Quasi-random samples were drawn using the Ax platform's
Sobol generator, which provides low-discrepancy coverage of the joint
search space and avoids the structured artefacts of pure random
sampling.

\subsection{CrabNet training and execution environment}
\label{sec:methods-execution}

For each sampled combination, CrabNet v2.0.8
(\url{https://github.com/sparks-baird/CrabNet}) was trained on each of
the five Matbench~\cite{Existing10_Dunn2020_Matbench} folds of the
\texttt{matbench\_expt\_gap} composition-only experimental band gap task
(\url{https://matbench.materialsproject.org/}). Python orchestration in
\texttt{scripts/crabnet\_hyperparameter/crabnet\_hyperparameter\_submitit.py}
dispatched jobs to SLURM via Submitit on the University of Utah's
Center for High-performance Computing (CHPC). To improve cluster
utilisation and reduce latency, hyperparameter sets (including
repeats) were shuffled and divided into batches, then submitted for
asynchronous evaluation. Per-run results --- the four observed metrics
plus the full input hyperparameter vector and provenance information
--- were streamed to a free-tier shared MongoDB Atlas collection via
the MongoDB Data API. Walltime and pre-emption limits caused
$\approx$47\% of scheduled runs not to complete; the trade-off was
considered acceptable for the throughput gains and is reflected in the
distribution of successful repeats per parameter set
(Section~\ref{sec:tech-validation}).

\subsection{Aggregation and percentile ranking}
\label{sec:methods-aggregation}

Successful runs were exported from MongoDB and aggregated to the
machine-learning-ready table described in Section~\ref{sec:data-records}.
For each repeated parameter set we additionally computed within-group
percentile ranks of MAE, RMSE, and runtime using the ``dense''
ranking method with \texttt{pct=True} in
\texttt{pandas.core.groupby.GroupBy.rank}. These percentile ranks
provide a non-parametric handle on heteroskedastic noise: rather than
imposing an a-priori Gaussian noise model parameterised by mean and
standard deviation, downstream surrogate models can sample from the
empirical rank distribution within a parameter cell, faithfully
reproducing the true (often heavy-tailed) noise structure of the
underlying training process. The rank-augmented table is the canonical
input for the surrogate models distributed alongside the dataset.

\section{Technical Validation}\label{sec:tech-validation}

The dataset's suitability as a noisy, multi-fidelity, multi-objective
benchmark rests on three properties of the raw runs: a meaningful
number of within-cell repeats, broad coverage of each output metric,
and an empirical (rather than imposed) noise distribution that varies
with the operating point. The histograms in
Figures~\ref{fig:repeats}--\ref{fig:size} demonstrate each property
in turn.

Figure~\ref{fig:repeats} shows that, although the cap was five repeats
per parameter set, the realised distribution of successful repeats is
broad and right-skewed, with a mean of $\approx$2.6 successful repeats
per set and 1--5 repeats observed across the 41{,}550 unique sets.
This is the empirical signature of cluster pre-emption rather than a
modelling assumption, and is precisely what makes the dataset a
faithful multi-fidelity testbed: the same input can be queried at
different effective fidelities (number of repeats), and the
realised-fidelity distribution itself is heterogeneous across the
search space.

Figures~\ref{fig:mae} and~\ref{fig:rmse} show that MAE and RMSE both
span more than two orders of magnitude across the explored search
space, with long upper tails dominated by configurations that train
unstable or under-parameterised models. This breadth ensures that
optimization algorithms have to discriminate between qualitatively
different regimes (well-trained, under-trained, divergent), not only
between marginally different ``good'' configurations.
Figure~\ref{fig:gpu} (log-scaled) shows a similarly wide GPU-runtime
distribution spanning roughly three orders of magnitude, which
underpins the dataset's multi-fidelity character: cheap configurations
can be used as low-fidelity proxies for expensive ones in
multi-fidelity acquisition functions
(refs~\cite{Existing01_Ghoreishi2019, SabanzaGil2025_MFBO,
Gantzler2023_MFBO_COF}). Figure~\ref{fig:size} confirms that on-disk
model size, the secondary objective, is also broadly distributed and
non-trivially correlated with accuracy, providing a meaningful Pareto
trade-off for multi-objective evaluation.

The five-fold cross-validation surrogate models distributed with the
dataset (\texttt{cross\_validation\_models\_\{0--4\}.pkl}, see
Section~\ref{sec:data-records}) provide a held-out estimate of how
faithfully the surrogate reproduces the underlying CrabNet response
surface. Together, the repeat structure and the percentile-rank
augmentation (Section~\ref{sec:methods-aggregation}) allow downstream
users to draw heteroskedastic noise samples that match the empirical
distribution within a parameter cell, rather than imposing an
a-priori Gaussian noise model.

\section{Usage Notes}\label{sec:usage}

The primary table \texttt{sobol\_regression.csv} can be loaded
directly with \texttt{pandas.read\_csv} for exploratory analysis. To
use the dataset as a benchmark for adaptive design, we recommend the
trained surrogate (\texttt{surrogate\_models.pkl}) instead: it
exposes a callable that maps any in-bounds 23-dimensional
hyperparameter vector to predicted MAE, RMSE, runtime and model size,
together with an empirical noise sample drawn from the within-cell
percentile-rank distribution. This avoids the cost of re-training
CrabNet (387 GPU-days for the full table) while preserving the
realistic, heteroskedastic noise structure that motivates the
benchmark.

The same surrogate is also exposed as an interactive HuggingFace
Space (see footnote in Section~\ref{sec:background}) and as a
REST-callable model that has been used as the objective in BayBE and
Ax benchmarking notebooks at
\url{https://github.com/AccelerationConsortium/baybe-multi-task-bo}
and as the objective for an Acceleration Consortium Kaggle
competition. These deployments are not part of the formal data record
but are useful starting templates for downstream users wishing to
benchmark a new acquisition function or optimisation library against
the dataset.

\section{Code availability}\label{sec:code}

All code used to generate, aggregate, and surrogate-fit the dataset
is openly available. The dispatch and aggregation pipeline lives in
the \texttt{matsci-opt-benchmarks} repository under
\url{https://github.com/sparks-baird/matsci-opt-benchmarks/tree/main/scripts/crabnet_hyperparameter}
and the data-preparation, surrogate-fitting and Zenodo-upload
notebooks under
\url{https://github.com/sparks-baird/matsci-opt-benchmarks/tree/main/notebooks/crabnet_hyperparameter}.
The exact source state used for the dataset is archived at Zenodo as
release v0.2.1 with DOI
\texttt{10.5281/zenodo.7694289}\footnote{\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7694289}}.
The forked CrabNet implementation actually executed at training time
is at \url{https://github.com/sparks-baird/CrabNet} (version 2.0.8).
